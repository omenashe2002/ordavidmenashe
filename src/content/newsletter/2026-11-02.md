This summer I worked at Fidelity National Financial, a company whose claim to fame, for most people, is being confused with Fidelity Investments. That is unfortunate for FNF, because it is the largest title insurer in the country, with roughly a third of the market by one estimate ([Rational Walk](https://rationalwalk.com/title-insurance/)). Title insurance is a financial product most people never think about until closing day: it insures that your title is yours. And it works backwards. Most insurance protects you from what might happen next; a title policy protects you, for a one-time premium, from defects that already exist when you buy and that nobody caught ([FNF 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001331875/000133187512000018/fnf12311110-k.htm)). A forged signature from 1987. A lien no one recorded properly. The house was never fully the seller's to sell, and you find out years later.

The value of title insurance, and of insurance by and large, is simple. When things go wrong, you need protection, and you need it to be there before you know you need it. Two stories from last week teach the same lesson from opposite ends. Both are about title: whether the things you hold are actually yours, and who is standing behind them when the defect surfaces.

## Twenty minutes

On the evening of October 29, an attacker moved about $6.36 billion out of Binance wallets on ten blockchains, most of it in roughly twenty minutes ([BleepingComputer](https://bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/binance-links-63-billion-crypto-theft-to-compromised-devops-laptop)). It is the largest crypto theft on record, about four times the $1.5 billion taken from Bybit in February 2025 ([Wealth Professional](https://www.wealthprofessional.ca/news/industry-news/binance-hack-63-billion-stolen-in-largest-crypto-heist-in-history/393908)). The FBI attributed it to TraderTraitor, the North Korean unit behind Bybit ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/binance-crypto-heist-withdrawals/)).

The attack was not a bolt from the blue. Binance traced it to a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop that gave the attacker access to systems able to authorize withdrawals, and on Ethereum the attacker held valid, signed authorizations that let a Binance wallet hand control to malicious code, apparently without any private key being stolen ([Bankless](https://www.bankless.com/read/binance-just-suffered-the-biggest-hack-in-crypto-history-what-now)). The attacker's wallet started receiving small withdrawals from Tornado Cash on the morning of October 28, and the code was rehearsed on Binance wallets hours before it ran ([Wealth Professional](https://www.wealthprofessional.ca/news/industry-news/binance-hack-63-billion-stolen-in-largest-crypto-heist-in-history/393908)). Like a bad deed, the defect was in the chain before anyone looked.

Then something unusual happened: nothing collapsed. Binance's dedicated user fund, SAFU, holds around $1 billion. Binance earmarked more than $6.34 billion to cover the loss, $5 billion of it already held in the affected tokens ([Bankless](https://www.bankless.com/read/binance-just-suffered-the-biggest-hack-in-crypto-history-what-now)). By Sunday it had replaced about 95% of the stolen assets and reopened withdrawals in phases ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/01/binance-resumes-withdrawals-following-6-3-billion-breach/)). Customers pulled a net $3.64 billion that day, about 2.1% of the exchange's $171 billion in assets ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/02/binance-outflow-hack-bitcoin.html)). A run that never became a run.

Look closely at that chart. The protection everyone pointed to, the insurance fund, covered about a sixth of the loss. What actually held was the balance sheet behind it, and a decision by Binance to spend it. That decision was voluntary.

The recovery has its own lesson. About $3 billion of the stolen funds was frozen within hours, including more than 800 million USDC frozen by Circle in coordination with law enforcement ([ForkLog](https://forklog.com/en/?p=106063)). What the attacker kept was bitcoin and ether, more than 420,000 ETH and 18,270 BTC, assets no issuer can freeze ([Wealth Professional](https://www.wealthprofessional.ca/news/industry-news/binance-hack-63-billion-stolen-in-largest-crypto-heist-in-history/393908)). The money that came back was the money someone could stop. The money that got away was the money no one could. Assets no one can freeze are supposed to be the safe kind; last Thursday, they were the kind that disappeared.

## Is it yours?

Here is the title question worth asking. When you hold crypto on an exchange, do you own the coins, or does the exchange owe you coins?

Those are different things, and the difference only matters on the worst day. In 2022, Coinbase told investors in its quarterly filing that in a bankruptcy, the crypto it holds for customers could become part of the estate, leaving those customers as general unsecured creditors ([Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2022/05/get-in-line-in-bankruptcy-court-crypto-assets-could-get-tied-up-in-crypto-custodians-bankruptcy)). Then a court tested it. On January 4, 2023, Judge Martin Glenn held that the terms of use Celsius customers clicked through unambiguously transferred title to the crypto in their Earn accounts to Celsius, so those assets belonged to the bankruptcy estate, not to the customers ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2023/01/read-before-you-click-accept)). Celsius was a lender, not an exchange, but the lesson carries: the answer turned on a contract customers clicked through. Weil Gotshal's own restructuring blog walked readers through the ruling at the time ([Weil](https://restructuring.weil.com/cryptocurrency-issues/winter-wears-on-celsius-court-rules-that-certain-customer-deposits-are-property-of-the-bankruptcy-estate)), which brings me to the second story.

## Three weeks

Weil, Gotshal & Manges brought in $2.078 billion in revenue in 2025, paid its equity partners an average of $5.593 million, and employed almost 1,200 lawyers and around 1,000 other professionals ([David Lat](https://davidlat.substack.com/p/weil-gotshal-manges-biglaw-firm-failure-collapse-dissolution)). It represented the debtors in six of the ten largest bankruptcies in American history, Enron, GM and Lehman among them ([Lat](https://davidlat.substack.com/p/weil-gotshal-manges-biglaw-firm-failure-collapse-dissolution)). It had no debt ([Lat](https://davidlat.substack.com/p/weil-gotshal-manges-biglaw-firm-failure-collapse-dissolution)). On October 28, its management committee recommended that the partnership wind the firm down ([Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/economy/wall-street-law-firm-handled-enron-lehman-brothers-bankruptcies-plans-dissolve-nearly-century)).

How does a firm like that fail? The proximate cause was a handful of people. In September, Michael Aiello, Weil's highest-paid partner and the head of a corporate practice of more than 600 people, left for Cravath with five partners ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/article/weil-gotshals-collapse-highlights-the-risk-of-building-a-business-around-star-talent/)). Private equity leaders followed, including Christopher Machera and Doug Warner to Paul Weiss ([Lat](https://davidlat.substack.com/p/weil-gotshal-manges-biglaw-firm-failure-collapse-dissolution)). Merger talks with Willkie Farr ended on October 22, and by late October more than fifty partners had left or announced they were leaving in 2026 ([Henrico Dolfing](https://www.henricodolfing.ch/en/case-study-49-weil-gotshal-and-the-economics-of-a-partnership-run/)). Ramona Nee, named in March to succeed the firm's longtime leader, had told management in August that she would not take the job; most partners learned it in the last week of October ([David Lat](https://davidlat.substack.com/p/weil-gotshal-manges-biglaw-firm-failure-collapse-dissolution)). Dewey & LeBoeuf took three to four months to come apart in 2012. Weil took three to four weeks ([Lat](https://davidlat.substack.com/p/weil-gotshal-manges-biglaw-firm-failure-collapse-dissolution)).

Yale's John Morley has a name for what happened: a partner run. Law firm partners are both the owners and the people producing the revenue, so each departure damages the firm, which pushes the next partner out, exactly like depositors pulling cash from a bank ([Morley, *Why Law Firms Collapse*](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2580616)). Morley predicted years ago that a firm could collapse this way with no debt at all. Matt Levine put numbers to it last week ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/newsletters/2026-10-29/run-on-the-law-firm)). Take a $2 billion firm with $600 million of fixed costs and 250 partners. Let twenty rainmakers who each bring in $50 million walk out.

In that example, 92% of the partners stay, and their pay falls by more than two-thirds. Nobody has to be a villain for this to happen. If you think the rainmakers might leave, the rational move is to leave first, because the last partner out inherits the lease. Weil's New York lease alone reportedly carries $54 million a year in rent through 2034 ([Henrico Dolfing](https://www.henricodolfing.ch/en/case-study-49-weil-gotshal-and-the-economics-of-a-partnership-run/), citing the *Financial Times*).

And here, too, the defect was old. Weil's revenue grew about half as fast as its peer group's from 2008 to 2025; in 2025 it grew less than 3%, against more than 13% across the fifty largest American firms ([Henrico Dolfing](https://www.henricodolfing.ch/en/case-study-49-weil-gotshal-and-the-economics-of-a-partnership-run/), citing Bloomberg Law). Its profits per partner were real money, and about half what the market leaders paid.

A gap that size is an open invitation to every recruiter in Manhattan. Weil had a title problem: the firm's value sat in relationships that, legally and practically, belonged to the partners.

New York law says as much. When Thelen and Coudert Brothers collapsed, their bankruptcy trustees tried to recover the profits former partners earned finishing old client matters at new firms. In 2014 the New York Court of Appeals said no: pending hourly matters are not partnership property ([*In re Thelen*](https://www.nycourts.gov/reporter/3dseries/2014/2014_04879.htm)). In the court's words, a law firm "does not own a client or an engagement" ([ABA Journal](https://www.abajournal.com/news/article/defunct_law_firms_arent_entitled_to_claw_back_unfinished_business_profits_n)). The ethics rules point the same way; a firm generally cannot contract to stop a lawyer from leaving and practicing elsewhere ([ABA Model Rule 5.6](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_5_6_restrictions_on_rights_to_practice/)). The clients walk with whoever they trust. The partner who stays gets the lease.

Staying can cost more than the lease. After Dewey filed for bankruptcy, its former partners were asked to pay back part of what the firm had paid them in its final years; a judge approved a $71.5 million settlement in which each contributed between $5,000 and $3.5 million in exchange for protection from future suits ([ABA Journal](https://www.abajournal.com/news/article/retired_dewey_partners_say_judges_clawback_pact_ok_adds_insult_to_injury_ar)). Partners who refused were later sued individually ([ABI](https://dev.abi.org/node/219508)). Weil is attempting an orderly wind-down outside bankruptcy precisely to avoid that path, but whether it can depends on settling with its creditors ([Henrico Dolfing](https://www.henricodolfing.ch/en/case-study-49-weil-gotshal-and-the-economics-of-a-partnership-run/)).

Meanwhile the people with the least title are the ones with the least warning. Associates and staff have been told they will be paid for 90 days ([David Lat](https://davidlat.substack.com/p/weil-gotshal-manges-biglaw-firm-failure-collapse-dissolution)), and nearly 600 associates are entering the job market at once, including first-years who started in October ([Henrico Dolfing](https://www.henricodolfing.ch/en/case-study-49-weil-gotshal-and-the-economics-of-a-partnership-run/)). Palantir's CFO is openly recruiting the students who were supposed to be Weil summer associates ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/10/30/prominent-law-firm-unravelingbut-palantir-has-an-intriguing-offer-cfo/)). Ramona Nee, meanwhile, was reportedly in advanced talks to move to Kirkland with forty or more professionals ([Henrico Dolfing](https://www.henricodolfing.ch/en/case-study-49-weil-gotshal-and-the-economics-of-a-partnership-run/), citing Bloomberg Law). Same firm, two very different weeks.

## Same question, two answers

One Weil partner put the fear plainly to [David Lat](https://davidlat.substack.com/p/weil-gotshal-manges-biglaw-firm-failure-collapse-dissolution): "If Weil isn't safe, then what firm is safe?" [Bankless](https://www.bankless.com/read/binance-just-suffered-the-biggest-hack-in-crypto-history-what-now) asked the identical question about Binance the morning after the hack. The answer to both is the same: safety was never a property of the institution. It was a property of what stood behind it and who could leave.

Binance had a backstop larger than its loss, and its customers believed it would use it. Weil had no backstop at all. A partnership has no outside capital to absorb a shock; the owners are the shock absorbers, and every owner could walk. One institution lost $6.3 billion and, by Changpeng Zhao's count, kept 98% of its customers ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/02/binance-outflow-hack-bitcoin.html)). The other lost a few partners and lost everything.

We apply this logic to our money without thinking. We diversify our investments because we know any single holding can fail. Then we do the opposite with our working lives: we specialize and consolidate our work product inside one institution, because that is what produces value for our employers. Specialization matters. But Weil shows exactly who survives a run, and it was not the generalists. It was the specialists whose value was portable: partners with clients who would follow them, teams that could move intact. The exposed people were the ones whose value was real but belonged to the firm's name on the door.

So the lesson is portability. It is what a title examiner does before closing: figure out what is actually yours, and build a little of it outside the building.

## So what do you do with this?

If you are a law student or a junior lawyer, read your firm the way a lateral recruiter does. Partner departures in leadership roles, a stalled merger, a succession plan that quietly changes, profits per partner well below the firms poaching from it: Weil showed every one of those signals in public before the announcement. If you see them, do not wait out the 90 days. One recruiter's advice to Weil associates was to start looking on your own immediately, because the market is already flooded with your peers ([CNBC](https://cnbc.com/make-it/2026/10/31/weil-wind-down-career-advice.html)). And build title in things that travel: relationships with clients and partners, your own reputation, a body of work with your name on it.

If you are a partner, or will be, read your partnership agreement for what happens on dissolution, who owns unfinished matters, and what capital you have in the firm. In New York the law favors the lawyer who leaves with a client, and the bill favors the one who stays (see *Thelen* and Dewey above). That asymmetry is the engine of every partner run.

If you hold crypto on an exchange, read the terms of use for one word: title. Some agreements say your assets remain yours, and institutional custody accounts are sometimes held in trust ([Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2022/05/get-in-line-in-bankruptcy-court-crypto-assets-could-get-tied-up-in-crypto-custodians-bankruptcy)); others, like Celsius's Earn terms, transferred ownership to the platform. Know which you signed. Ask whether the exchange publishes proof of reserves and how large its insurance fund is relative to what it holds. And understand the trade you make with self-custody: you remove the exchange's title risk, and you take on all of the security risk Binance just paid $6.3 billion to absorb.

If you run a business, find your Aiello. Every company has one: the client who is a third of revenue, the vendor you cannot replace, the exchange holding your treasury, the one person whose departure takes others with them. Weil's two best-paid partners each earned more than $20 million a year ([Lat](https://davidlat.substack.com/p/weil-gotshal-manges-biglaw-firm-failure-collapse-dissolution), citing the *Wall Street Journal*), and the money did not hold the firm together, because the problem was the dependence itself. Spread it before someone else spreads it for you.

A fair reader will push back, and should. Most firms survive partner exodus; Boies Schiller, K&L Gates and O'Melveny all weathered waves of departures that observers called fatal, and Weil itself survived one in 2013 and 2014 ([David Lat](https://davidlat.substack.com/p/weil-gotshal-manges-biglaw-firm-failure-collapse-dissolution)). And Binance is arguably proof that custodial crypto works: the system had a defect, the backstop held, and customers were made whole within days. I read both as consistent with the point. The institutions that survive are the ones whose backstop is larger than the bad day. Most of us never check.

## What to watch

Weil's partners still have to formally vote on the wind-down; as of today that vote has not been confirmed ([Henrico Dolfing](https://www.henricodolfing.ch/en/case-study-49-weil-gotshal-and-the-economics-of-a-partnership-run/)), and its London office is expected to close December 31 ([Non-Billable](https://www.nonbillable.co.uk/news/weil-set-to-dissolve-leadership-recommends-wind-down)). Watch whether creditors accept a wind-down outside bankruptcy, and where the remaining practice groups land. Watch for Binance's full root cause; it has not yet explained how the attacker moved from one compromised machine to signed withdrawals ([SOCRadar](https://socradar.io/blog/binance-hack-6-3b-hot-wallet-theft/)). And on November 11, MSCI publishes its final list of companies it no longer considers operating businesses, which could push Strategy and other crypto treasury companies out of its indexes by November 30 ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/news/business/2026-10-15-msci-rules-could-exclude-strategy-mstr-from-indexes-418729)). It is the same question one more time: what does a company actually hold, and is it what the market thought?

Now you know.
