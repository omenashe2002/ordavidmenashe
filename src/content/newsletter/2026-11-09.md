Every couple of months I lose the same fight.

I set Opal, an app that locks the rest of my phone, for a 9-hour block. The plan is simple: get through the day's work without the phone pulling at me. I haven't made it even two weeks.

Somewhere in the first day or two, I need to get into Chase to send a Zelle. Or I need to check my schedule in Google Calendar. Or I just want to listen to music. At first, I go through the same hassle of getting into the app, taking a 5-second breath, and then permitting Opal to "Take a Break," which lasts 15 minutes. Yes, it's a hard limit and will shut down your app mid-use if the time runs out. By the two-week mark, I delete Opal.

The phone stays. Opal goes.

I am not alone in this. Americans check their phones [186 times a day](https://natlawreview.com/press-releases/cell-phone-usage-2026-americans-average-186-phone-checks-day), roughly once every five minutes they are awake; about 85% check within ten minutes of waking up, and three in four feel uneasy leaving the phone at home. [More than half](https://www.ksl.com/article/51423925) say they have never gone a full 24 hours without it. Opal says its users have saved [over 500 million hours](https://opalapp.com/blog/opal-saved-humans-over-500-million-hours) since launch. I believe it. I have also deleted Opal more times than I would like to admit.

For a while I treated this as a willpower problem.

## The web

In 1951, W.V.O. Quine published "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," and in it he made a claim that we don't actually test all of our beliefs at the same time (even if we think we do). Everything we believe, from where we left our keys to the laws of logic, forms a single web, and the web touches experience only at its edges. When an experience conflicts with the web, something has to give, but the experience does not tell you what. You choose. Either the new belief is rejected, or the web is altered in light of this change.

How do you choose? Quine answered that we disturb the web as little as possible; he later called it the maxim of minimum mutilation. Beliefs at the edge, the ones close to experience and tied to little else, get revised first. Beliefs at the center, like math and logic, get protected, because pulling one of them out drags the whole web with it. He wrote that "no statement is immune to revision," and he meant it: he pointed to proposals to give up the logical law of the excluded middle to make quantum mechanics simpler. Math can change. We seldom let it, because the cost of changing the center is everything else – but it can.

For roughly two thousand years, Euclid's geometry sat about as close to the center of the web as anything could. Parallel lines never meet; the angles of a triangle add up to 180 degrees. Kant thought this was so certain that it was built into the way the mind organizes space. Then, in the nineteenth century, Lobachevsky, Bolyai and Riemann worked out geometries where the parallel postulate fails, where a triangle's angles add up to more or less than 180 degrees. In 1915, Einstein's general theory of relativity used Riemann's geometry to describe gravity itself: mass curves spacetime, and the space we actually live in is not Euclidean. In 1919, Arthur Eddington's expedition photographed stars near the Sun during a total eclipse and found their light bent by close to the amount Einstein predicted.

While my issue looks different, it's really the same. The new belief, "I do not need my phone for three hours," sits at the edge. The phone sits at the center, wired into my school login, my bank, my calendar, my two-factor codes, and my family group chat. When the two conflict, minimum mutilation picks the loser before I do. Opal is at the edge. The edge always loses.

## The center

The biologist E.O. Wilson once described the human problem this way: "We have Paleolithic emotions, medieval institutions, and godlike technology." It usually gets quoted as a warning about mismatch. Our instincts and our institutions cannot keep up with our tools.

I think the mismatch is the smaller problem. The bigger one is that the three are now wired together. My Paleolithic reflex to check for a threat or a message fires 186 times a day through a godlike machine, and that machine is run by a handful of institutions that decide what sits at the center of my web. Every tool added to the stack connects to the tools already there. Every connection makes the center heavier, and the heavier the center gets, the more it costs to revise anything near it and the more power sits with whoever controls it.

Quine's web was something you built slowly, from your own experience. The web on your phone was built for you. Someone else decides what goes in the middle.

## Opt out

This week Google showed what that looks like at scale.

On November 3, Google announced that starting in early 2027, its AI tools for K-12 students, including Gemini, NotebookLM and Gemini in Classroom, will be [off by default](https://techcrunch.com/?p=3184603) unless administrators turn them on. That sounds like a gift to schools. The catch is that schools that turn AI off entirely lose Google Search too. Google's vice president for education, Chris Phillips, told *The New York Times* that "Search and AI are deeply integrated," and that students at schools that restrict AI [will not have access to search](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/google-search-access-schools-ai-tools/).

Consider the size of the web Google is pulling on. Google handles about [90% of the world's searches](https://gs.statcounter.com/search-engine-market-share). Google Workspace for Education has more than [170 million users](https://www.content.shi.com/cms-content/accelerator/media/brands/google/google-for-education-onepager-012126.pdf), Google Classroom more than 150 million, and Chromebooks have been the top device in K-12 education since 2021. New York City and Los Angeles, the two largest districts in the country, have [temporary bans](https://www.ajc.com/news/2026/11/google-will-cut-off-search-access-for-students-at-schools-that-reject-ai-tools/) on generative AI in elementary and middle schools. Under Google's policy, a school that turns AI off entirely turns search off with it.

Google took AI, a new belief at the edge of every school's web, and tied it to search, which sits at the center. A school can still say no; it just has to give up the center to do it. Minimum mutilation does the rest. I expect most schools will keep search, which means most schools will keep AI. Google does not have to win the argument about AI in classrooms. It only has to make losing the argument more expensive than giving in.

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani accused Google of "trying to use its monopoly power to force AI into our public schools." Former FTC Chair Lina Khan wrote that Google "will punish those who don't fall in line." Lawyers will recognize the shape of the complaint: tying a product the customer may not want to one it cannot live without. Whether the policy survives an antitrust challenge is a separate question. Whether it works is not.

OKXICE, the joint venture between crypto exchange OKX and NYSE parent Intercontinental Exchange, planned to offer tokenized versions of about 62 large U.S. stocks. Under the SEC's framework, a company gets [30 calendar days](https://cryptobriefing.com/okxice-tokenized-stocks-issuer-objections/) after notice to object to its shares being tokenized. Companies objected. By the latest count, [59 have opted out](https://www.cryptopolitan.com/companies-reject-okxice-apple-opt-out/), including Apple, [Microsoft and Alphabet](https://pro.edgex.exchange/en-US/news/article/companies-block-tokenized-shares-okxice-venue). Nasdaq opted out its own stock too.

When Apple wants out of a new technology, it gets a 30-day window and a clean exit. When a school district wants out, it gets a choice between AI and search. Whether you can leave depends on how much the web needs you.

Connection has a second cost, and it is the one nobody prices.

On October 29, Binance lost about $6.3 billion in the largest crypto theft on record. Its incident report, released November 3, traced part of the attack to a job search. In September, a Binance engineer who was looking for a new job was approached on a [legitimate job-hunting platform](https://www.hcamag.com/ca/news/general/fake-job-interview-behind-us63bn-binance-heist-puts-hiring-on-notice/596049). A fake recruiter interviewed him on video. A fake CEO sent him a coding assignment. The assignment was malware. Weeks later, attackers used compromised machines, plus an [internal AI tool](https://www.deccanchronicle.com/amp/business/binance-hackers-used-fake-job-interview-in-63-billion-attack-2001637), to reach the exchange's wallets.

Think about what sat at the edge of that web: one employee's private job search. Think about what sat at the center: billions of dollars in customer funds. North Korea-linked groups took about [76% of all crypto hack value](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/cyber/binances-6-3bn-hack-began-with-a-fake-job-offer-and-it-had-no-cyber-cover-596035.aspx) in the first four months of 2026, according to TRM Labs, and fake recruiting is one of their best-documented methods. Binance says it blocked 57 suspected North Korea-linked attempts this year before this one got through. It also had no cyber insurance, a position it shares with many crypto firms; its own user protection fund held about $1 billion before the hack.

The wiring that makes my phone impossible to lock is the same wiring that let one job interview reach a $6.3 billion wallet.

## Walking out

The same wiring follows you to work, and it is hardest to unplug the day you leave.

Last week I wrote about Weil and the advice to build portable value: client relationships, reputation, the things that walk out the door with you. AI complicates that. In 8am's [2026 Legal Industry Report](https://www.8am.com/blog/ai-adoption-law-firms-2026-legal-industry-report/), 69% of legal professionals said they use generative AI for work, up from 31% a year earlier. Meanwhile, 43% of firms have no AI governance policy and 54% offer no AI training. Lawyers are building faster than their firms are writing rules.

What are they building? Prompt libraries, custom instructions, agents tuned to how a partner likes a memo, workflows that turn a three-day diligence task into an afternoon. More and more, that is where a junior lawyer's edge lives. The problem is where it sits. If it lives in the firm's AI platform, under the firm's account, it sits at the center of the firm's web. Most employment agreements assign the employer what you create on the job, and that language was [written for patents and code](https://moonfoundry.pages.dev/essays/who-owns-your-prompt-library/), not prompts. Read broadly, it covers them.

Your skill comes with you; general knowledge and skill have long been treated as the employee's own. The artifacts do not. The prompts, the agents and the tuned workflows stay behind, and so does the record of every prompt you wrote. Try to take them and you may be in a trade secret fight. Federal trade secret filings hit an [all-time high in 2025](https://www.seyfarth.com/dir_docs/documents/presentation-slides/260909-from-prompt-to-proof-investigating-and-litigating-trade-secret-theft-in-the-age-of-ai-slide.pdf), and firms are now training lawyers on cases where the alleged theft is an AI-generated plan that shares none of the original documents' language.

The more of your value lives inside a tool someone else controls, the more leaving costs you. Your judgment sits at your center. Your workflow sits at theirs.

## Rebuilding at sea

So can we unravel the web?

Quine borrowed an image from the philosopher Otto Neurath: we are like sailors who must rebuild our ship on the open sea, plank by plank, without ever bringing it into dry dock. "Delete Google," "throw out your phone," and "go off the grid" are all dry-dock advice. Nobody gets the dry dock. Whatever you change, you change while staying afloat.

You cannot pull the phone out of the center of your web this week. You can make the center lighter, one plank at a time, so the next conflict does not have to end with the edge losing. If my two-factor codes lived somewhere other than my phone, Opal would have one less reason to lose.

The solution (if we can call it that) is simple enough to formalize, but I'm the first to admit that it's difficult in practice. First, hold value that outlasts whatever you are locked into. Second, get very good at the lock-ins that will stay, and keep watching the edges, because those can and will move.

If you are a junior lawyer building workflows on your firm's AI, keep a private record of your judgment, not your prompts. After each matter, write down in your own words, with no client information, what the task was, where the model went wrong, and what you checked to catch it. The prompt belongs to the firm and will be stale in six months anyway. The judgment is yours; it is the part treated as general skill when you leave, and it is the part the next firm is actually paying for.

If you are early in your career and deciding what to master, bet on the centers that move slowly. The tools change every year. Delaware corporate law, the securities laws, the UCC and the way a credit agreement fits together change far more slowly. Learn your firm's AI platform well, because it will be your daily lock-in, but spend your hardest hours on the layer underneath it. When the platform changes, the person who knows the law and the deal still knows what good output looks like.

If you are about to sign an offer letter, read the IP assignment and confidentiality clauses now, not the week you resign. Ask whether "work product" covers prompts, agents and outputs, and whether personal AI accounts are allowed for anything. That question is easy to ask as a new hire and expensive to ask on the way out.

If you depend on any tool you did not choose, assume the edges will move. Google's school AI went from switched on by default in August to tied to search in November. Once a quarter, list the five tools your work cannot run without and mark each one center or edge. For every edge tool, know your exit before you need it: where the export button is and what you would switch to.

And if you are fighting your own version of my Opal problem, look at what you are locking. My lock kept failing because I aimed it at the center: Chase, my calendar, my music. Opal lets you choose what to block. Leave the center open, lock only the edge, and the lock holds.

## Pushback

The web is also why any of this works. My phone is hard to lock because it is useful, and a world where nothing connects is slower and worse. Quine would say the same about beliefs: a tightly connected web is more powerful because everything bears on everything else.

There is also a fair case for Google. AI really is part of search now, and maintaining a version of search with the AI surgically removed, for a product schools get at no cost, may not be worth it. I take that seriously. My answer is that connection by itself is fine. The question is who controls the center and what it costs to leave.

## What to watch

Google's new controls arrive in early 2027, with [testing in selected U.S. schools](https://mezha.ua/en/news/google-to-disable-search-in-schools-rejecting-ai-316850/) starting this month. Watch whether districts like New York City and Los Angeles push back, negotiate or switch providers, and whether the antitrust conversation picks this up.

Watch the OKXICE list. Fifty-nine opt-outs is a signal about what issuers think of third-party tokenization; the next question is whether companies start issuing tokenized shares on their own terms.

And watch your own center. The next time something forces you to choose between a tool at the edge and the phone in the middle, notice which one loses.

I already know which one loses for me - now maybe you will too.
