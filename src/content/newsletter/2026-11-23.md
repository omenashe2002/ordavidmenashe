Last week I opened the edited draft of my law journal note and found thirty-six comments in the margin. Seven of them were from October. They were short, lowercase, and a little rude in the way a good editor is rude: "Define." "???" They had been typed over about twenty minutes, one at a time, by someone reading.

The other thirty-one carried the same timestamp, down to the second. They averaged forty-four words each. They were polite, complete, and built on one frame: an observation, a suggestion, and the benefit to “readers.” I cannot prove a machine wrote them. I can say that nobody types thirty-one comments in one second.

Here is the part that bothered me, and it is not the part I expected. The comments were mostly right. Several tracked real features of the draft, down to a dollar figure in a footnote. I went through all thirty-one looking for an error and did not find one worth arguing about. Nearly every comment asked me to add something – a sentence, an example, a clarification. One suggested moving a paragraph. None suggested cutting anything, on a draft that already runs about 11,900 words, way longer than any other note. There was no serious engagement with the work; what was working on a metalevel; what matters most; what wasn’t working.

Thirty-one true statements, and I finished reading them knowing nothing new about my note. Is my work even good?

I am the senior notes editor of the journal publishing it, so I should say this plainly: this is not a story about one editor. On November 9 the statistician Jessica Hullman asked whether peer review can survive reviewers’ unrestricted use of AI ([Statistical Modeling](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2026/11/09/can-peer-review-survive-unrestricted-ai-use-by-reviewers/)). She reports that at ICML 2026, one of the largest machine learning conferences, reviewers who had volunteered for the no-AI pool were caught using it anyway, through watermarks planted for the purpose. Some reviewers, she writes, now tell authors outright that they used a tool because they did not have the time.

The people trusted to check the work are handing the checking to a machine. And the checks come back correct. But what is the value of the “correctness”?

## Since 2022

I have no standing to be smug about this. I have used these tools since 2022, and for a stretch I used them the way those comments were made. I handed over the work and kept the output. In a bind I want to obfuscate. I would love to hand over all my busy work, the “too much on my plate assignment,” the shit I just don’t feel like doing to AI.

I still use them. I brainstormed this issue with one, and it argued with me about the structure. The difference I care about now is how a user makes the active decision to stay involved in the process; how we choose to hold on to the parts that AI may not do as well (at least or now). It's really about the worst word in corporate culture: Collaboration.

That difference globally is what the rest of this issue is about, because this month it stopped being a private habit and became the biggest story in mathematics.

## Seventeen million lines

On Friday, November 20, a mathematician at Anthropic named Levent Alpöge posted a four-page note on his personal website. Its central sentence: “I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis” ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03812-7)).

The Riemann hypothesis dates to 1859. It is a claim about where the zeros of one function fall, and through them a claim about how the prime numbers are spaced out to infinity. A million-dollar prize has sat unclaimed on it since 2000. It is the problem mathematicians name when asked for the most important one they cannot solve.

Alpöge did not publish a paper. He published code: about 17 million lines in Lean, a language in which a computer checks every logical step ([Science](https://www.science.org/content/article/ai-may-have-solved-math-s-greatest-mystery-can-humans-understand-answer)). By Saturday the Lean and Mathlib communities had certified that the statement was the real hypothesis and that the proof holds ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/anthropic-mathematician-claims-to-prove-riemann-hypothesis-most-famous-problem-in-all-of-math/)). Dozens of people compiled it independently. Checked, in about a day.

Understanding is a separate matter. A human-readable version of the argument would reportedly run past 250,000 pages, and it has not been released. Alpöge has said he is still working to understand what he posted. So the most famous open question in mathematics now has an answer that is certified true, and as of this morning no person can say why.

Jason Gottlieb, a digital assets litigator whose posts I read closely, wrote about this over the weekend. His argument is that an AI proof matters only as a tool for understanding why the proof works. We have had answer machines for decades, he points out; a calculator will give you fifteen percent of the bill and nobody mourns arithmetic. The answer, he wrote, "is just the answer." The reasoning is the thing worth having.

I think he is right about the reasoning and a little too easy on the machine. A calculator runs a procedure that people write and understand. This found something no person had found since 1859. And the bare answer is worth a great deal: a large body of number theory was proved on the assumption that Riemann holds, and those results now stand on something firmer than hope.

## Eighty-six percent

Why do we run with it? Because running with it is the correct decision almost every time.

On October 27, Anthropic published a randomized trial ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/10/28/experts-defer-to-ai-in-decision-making-anthropic-study-finds)). Fifty professional analysts used an AI agent to advise a fictional farming cooperative on four judgment calls, each with two defensible answers. A hidden coin flip decided which answer each agent would lean toward. The analysts chose their agent's lean 86 percent of the time; with no influence the figure would be 50. Analysts who had stated their own preference first abandoned it 60 percent of the time when the agent disagreed ([Harvard Kennedy School](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/centers/carr-ryan/our-work/carr-ryan-commentary/experts-use-ai-drift-deference-or-mindful)). Most then justified the choice in the agent's own arguments, and only 15 percent credited the agent. The study covers one task and one model, and it comes from the company that makes the tool I used to brainstorm this issue. I still find it hard to wave away. These were experts, on their own ground, and a coin flip made the call.

The second half of the loop is the one that flatters us. A study in *Science* this March tested eleven leading models and found that they endorsed a user's problematic behavior 47 percent of the time ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/23/nx-s1-5792867/ai-chatbot-flattery-mental-health-risks)). People preferred the agreeable versions and trusted them more. When I know less than the machine, I defer to it. When I think I know more, it defers to me. Either way no one pushes back, and the output looks fine.

Researchers at USC argued this spring that people who polish their writing with a chatbot lose their stylistic individuality ([USC Dornsife](https://dornsife.usc.edu/news/stories/ai-may-be-making-us-think-and-write-more-alike/)). A group studying what they call the lock-in hypothesis describes the full circle: the models learn our beliefs, hand them back reinforced, and then train on the reinforced version ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.06166v1)). My reading over the past month has felt like that. The same five ideas, each time in slightly better prose.

Each single act of deference is rational, and the reason we got call out the obfuscation is because it proves what we are all afraid of: we are in The Age of Human Obsolescence

## What it does better

These tools beat me at most of what writing gets graded on. They are more accurate than I am on a first pass, they cover more ground, they organize faster, and they do not get tired at eleven at night. If an editor's job is to find every place a draft could say more, the thirty-one comments did that job better than I would have.

What I think people still do better is narrower: sound, and creativity of a specific kind. I do not mean that a model cannot write a good sentence. It can. I mean that it writes the likely sentence, and a voice is made of unlikely ones. The research is consistent on this in an interesting way: people using AI produce ideas that score well one at a time and look alike as a group ([PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/real-world-evidence-shows-generative-ai-is-making-human-creative-output-more-uniform/)). The average rises. The outliers disappear. If the machine is better at most things and gaining on the rest, how does a person hold value at all?

## Says nothing

Ludwig Wittgenstein finished the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* in 1918 while serving in the Austrian army, and it was published in 1921. It is a short book about the limits of what can be said. I did not expect a hundred-year-old book on logic to explain my margin comments. However…it did.

The first is the tautology. A proposition, for Wittgenstein, earns its meaning by taking a risk: it is true if the world is one way and false if the world is another. A tautology takes no risk. "It is raining or it is not raining" is true in every possible world, and for that reason, he says at 4.461, it says nothing.

Read the thirty-one comments again with that in mind. "You might add an example here." "It may help readers to clarify this term." Those sentences are true of my draft. They are also true of every draft that has ever been written, including this one. A comment that would be correct on any page tells you nothing about the page it is on. The seven October comments were the opposite. "???" next to a paragraph can be wrong. That is why it meant something.

The second idea comes near the end of the book. At 6.41 Wittgenstein says that value is not found among the facts of the world: everything in the world is as it is, and no arrangement of facts adds up to why any of it matters. At 6.52 he draws the consequence. Even if every possible scientific question were answered, he writes, the problems of life "have still not been touched at all."

It is easy to read that as mysticism. This week it reads like a news summary. A scientific question, arguably the scientific question, was answered on a Friday. It is checked. And the thing mathematicians wanted from it, the understanding, has not been touched at all.

Obsolescence is a claim about facts: this system produces more correct output than that person. If your value was your output, the claim lands, and no essay will save you. But the comments in my margin were all output and held no value, and the proof on Alpöge's website is all truth and holds, for now, no understanding. Truth and value were separate before any of this started. The machines made the gap impossible to miss.

So the answer I can defend is small. A person holds value by being the one who can say why, and who answers for it. My hackathon paper this fall asked who pays when an AI agent pays the wrong party, and the answer in every version was that a person or an institution has to stand behind the act. The same holds for a proof, an edit, or a brief. Someone has to own the why.

## So what do you do with this?

If you are a student or a junior anything, draft before you prompt. Get your own bad version on the page first, then bring in the tool to argue with it. The order matters more than the amount. The analysts in the Anthropic study who stated a preference first still switched most of the time, but they at least had something to switch from.

If you edit or review other people's work, rank. Anyone, and anything, can produce thirty-one suggestions. Tell the writer the three biggest problems and what you would cut. Make comments that could be wrong. If you cannot, you have not read the piece yet.

If you are on the receiving end of feedback, ask those two questions back: what are the three biggest problems, and what would you cut? A reader who understood the draft can answer in a minute.

If you sign things, whether briefs, opinions, audits, or code reviews, treat your signature as a claim that you can explain why. Use the tool for whatever it does better. Before your name goes on, close it and say the argument out loud.

If you use these tools to think, and I do, ask for the strongest case against your position, and read something written before 2022 once in a while. The loop cannot hand you what it has never seen.

## Pushback

A fair reader will object that civilization has always run on answers nobody understands. Alfred North Whitehead wrote in 1911 that it advances by extending the number of operations we can perform without thinking about them. I cannot explain my phone, my computer, and most tech I rely on. And mathematics has been here before: the four color theorem was proved with a computer in 1976, mathematicians argued for years about whether that counted, and the field survived.

The evidence on thinking is also less one-sided than I have made it sound. A meta-analysis published this month in *Smart Learning Environments* carries its conclusion in its title: motivational gain trumps cognitive offloading ([Smart Learning Environments](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40561-026-00477-7)). Across the studies it pooled, AI use had a net positive effect on critical thinking, with the gain running through learners' motivation.

My reply to the first is that the old chain of deference ended in a person. I do not understand my phone, but an engineer does. In 1976 a mathematician could at least describe the strategy the computer was carrying out. This time the author of the proof says he is still working to understand it. The chain may, for the first time, end in no one, but in AI. My reply to the second is that it describes people who use the tool to learn.

## What to watch

First, whether a readable account of the Riemann proof appears, and how long it takes; Science expects the deciphering to take years. Second, NeurIPS meets in Sydney in December under a rule that bars reviewers from uploading papers to chatbots, after organizers hid prompts in submissions to catch the ones who do ([The Scientist](https://www.the-scientist.com/a-trap-for-ai-use-in-peer-reviews-sparks-controversy-74702)). Third, the Clay Mathematics Institute holds the million-dollar prize, and I have not found a statement on whether a proof that exists only as code can win it.

As for my note: I am going to edit it myself, starting with the real objections.
