In 1974, after 121 publishers had turned it down, William Morrow published a long, strange book by a former writing teacher who paid his bills writing technical manuals. The editor warned Robert Pirsig not to expect more than his $3,000 advance. *Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance* sold 50,000 copies in three months and more than 5 million since ([AP](https://www.bangordailynews.com/2017/04/25/news/robert-pirsig-author-of-counterculture-classic-zen-and-the-art-of-motorcycle-maintenance-dies-at-88/)). Its subject is a word everyone uses and almost no one can define: quality. Pirsig came to it as a teacher at Montana State College, where he asked his writing students to define quality and spent the rest of his life on what followed ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pirsig%27s_Metaphysics_of_Quality)). His answer, roughly, is that we recognize quality long before we can say what it is.

Early in the book, Pirsig takes his motorcycle to a shop. The mechanics are young, a radio is going full blast, and the one who finally walks over barely listens to the engine before naming the problem. He is wrong. Two weeks and $140 later, the engine seizes again at about 75 miles an hour ([excerpt](https://www.cyclechat.net/threads/bad-professional-mechanics.161479/latest)). Looking back, Pirsig decides the radio was a clue: you cannot think hard about a job and listen to the radio at the same time. His verdict on the mechanics was four words. "They were like spectators" ([excerpt](https://mannerofspeaking.org/?p=13489)).

I have been thinking about those mechanics all week, because the rest of the economy is catching up to them. More people than ever are lining up for training that's supposed to make them valuable, but the work is being handed to machines. And courts are beginning to decide who answers for what the machines produce. We have a quality problem, and it reaches well past AI.

Law school has not been this popular since 2010. The Law School Admission Council counted 82,487 applicants in the 2026 cycle, up 7.9% from the year before and 27.4% from two years earlier ([LSAC](https://www.lsac.org/podcast/keeping-data-september-2026)), and the next cycle is already running 12% ahead of this one ([LSAC](https://www.lsac.org/blog/early-2027-data-show-continuing-strong-interest-law-school)). The reasons are not mysterious. Recent college graduates faced unemployment of about 5.6% in the second quarter, and roughly 42% were underemployed ([Modern Counsel](https://modern-counsel.com/2026/record-law-school-demand-tests-the-us-legal-talent-pipeline/)); one admissions consultant attributes most of the surge to hiring woes ([Fortune](https://dc.fortune.com/2026/01/26/is-it-worth-it-to-go-to-law-school-ai-hiring-entry-level)). Business school tells a quieter version of the same story. Applications rose 7% globally in 2025, though they fell 1% in the United States ([GMAC](https://www.gmac.com/resources/learners/business-programs/explore-programs/students-prioritize-in-person-degrees-mba-applications-rise-again)), and fewer candidates now enroll to switch careers, 42% compared with 58% in 2022, while more enroll to close specific skill gaps or wait out a downturn ([GMAC](https://www.gmac.com/why-gmac/gmac-news/gmac-press-releases/global-talent-on-the-move)). Graduate school has become a shelter.

Employers keep telling the National Association of Colleges and Employers the same thing. In its 2025 survey, 96.1% called critical thinking very or extremely important in new hires, yet only 55.9% rated recent graduates as very or extremely proficient in it. Communication shows the same gap, 96.1% against 53.5% ([NACE](https://www.naceweb.org/career-readiness/competencies/the-gap-in-perceptions-of-new-grads-competency-proficiency-and-resources-to-shrink-it); [Pledge a Smile](https://pledgeasmile.com/guides/the-two-skills-employers-call-most-important)). The graduates see themselves differently: 80.8% rate their own critical thinking as proficient. Employers are hiring for judgment, and judgment is the one thing a credential cannot show.

Why is the gap widening now? Because junior work is the work AI does best. In an MIT experiment published in *Science*, 453 professionals wrote press releases, short reports, analysis plans and delicate emails; those given ChatGPT finished 40% faster and produced work graders rated 18% higher in quality, and the weakest writers gained the most ([Noy and Zhang](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Noy_Zhang_1_0.pdf); [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2023/07/13/1076199/chatgpt-can-turn-bad-writers-into-better-ones/amp/)). That study used 2023 tools. Employers have acted on it since. ZipRecruiter found that 38% of employers have moved basic data entry and processing from entry-level workers to AI, and 31% have raised experience requirements for entry-level jobs as a result ([ZipRecruiter](https://www.ziprecruiter-research.org/economic-insights-research/ai-employer-report-2026)). A Stanford study of payroll records found that workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations saw a 16% relative decline in employment, while experienced workers in the same jobs held steady or grew ([Stanford Digital Economy Lab](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publications/canaries-in-the-coal-mine)). And on October 16, Anthropic reported that juniors fell from 27% to 9% of its technical hires, with its managers now screening for autonomy and judgment over technical expertise ([AI Weekly](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/how-employment-is-changing-at-anthropic)).

PwC's 2026 jobs barometer puts a number on the paradox. Entry-level roles exposed to AI are being rewritten to demand traditionally senior capabilities, like strategic decision making and team building, which now make up 52% of the new skills those jobs require; for the least exposed roles, the figure is 7% ([PwC](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/issues/artificial-intelligence/job-barometer/2026/2026-global-ai-jobs-barometer-full-report.pdf)). Employers want juniors who already have judgment, while handing the first drafts, the document review and the data cleanup that used to build judgment to software. Where is a junior supposed to learn what good looks like if the first draft is no longer theirs to write?

None of this means AI does sloppy work. This month it did something more unsettling: it found mistakes the experts had missed. PostgreSQL, one of the most carefully reviewed open-source projects in the world, pushed its next major release from early October to around November 19 and pulled 8 to 10 significant features after beta began ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/databases/2026/10/16/postgres-19-delayed-after-ai-reviewers-find-more-bugs-than-humans-can-fix/5303471)). Peter Eisentraut, a member of the project's core team, wrote that LLM-assisted code review surfaced defects far faster than before, leaving developers with a list of roughly forty defect reports ([Technology.org](https://www.technology.org/2026/10/19/postgresql-19-delayed-llm-code-review-reverts/)). The flawed code was written by people, long before AI review was any good; the machines raised the bar after the fact ([daily.dev](https://daily.dev/posts/eisentraut-the-reverts-will-continue-until-morale-improves-1b6fogwm2)). Some committers blame late human review for part of the delay ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/gabrielanhaia/postgres-19-reverted-8-to-10-features-and-now-targets-nov-19-was-ai-review-the-cause-2fnb)), but the lesson holds either way. Finding problems got cheap. Deciding which ones matter did not.

Writing shows the same split. AI prose is very good at being acceptable. A study of more than 15 million biomedical abstracts found that certain style words spiked so abruptly after ChatGPT arrived that at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts were likely processed by a language model ([Kobak et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07016v5)). A study in *Nature Human Behaviour* found that when a model polishes human writing, the meaning survives and the writer disappears: rewrites cut the variance in writing complexity by 21% to 50% and erased the signals researchers use to infer a writer's age, gender and personality ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.11266); [AI Weekly](https://aiweekly.co/node/10775)). Corporate writing rewards exactly that. It wants prose that is clear, predictable and safe to forward, and AI delivers it on demand. The paragraph below is the kind now filling inboxes.

Nothing in that paragraph is wrong, and that is the problem. When every memo, cover letter and client update reads the same, the writing stops telling the reader anything about the writer. A polished application used to signal a skilled applicant. Today it signals access to a tool.

If AI output is this competent, why do we still need people at all? Two German courts answered from the other direction. In May, the Munich Regional Court held Google directly liable after its AI Overviews tied two publishers to fraud and subscription traps, claims that appeared in none of the sources it cited; Google appealed, then settled before a higher court could rule ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/two-german-courts-now-hold-google-liable-for-what-its-ai-overviews-say/)). On September 24, in a decision made public October 18, the Aachen Regional Court went further. Google's AI Mode had told users that a local doctor, the former head of a neuroradiology clinic, had been convicted of a sex offense. The conviction belonged to someone else. The doctor asked Google to take it down in late August, the text stayed up, and the court enjoined Google without hearing from it first, with fines of up to €250,000 per violation ([Dealroom](https://dealroom.co/news/164415-german-courts-rule-google-liable-for-fabrications-in-its-ai-search/); [Mad Robot](https://madrobot.blog/2026/10/18/german-courts-google-liable-ai-overviews-ai-mode-aachen-munich-ruling/)).

Both courts reasoned that an AI answer is the company's own statement. The model combines third-party material into something independent, new and substantive, so whoever deploys it owns what it says ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/two-german-courts-now-hold-google-liable-for-what-its-ai-overviews-say/)). Aachen added a detail every professional should sit with: had Google put the doctor's objection to its own AI, the system would have caught the error. These are preliminary rulings from regional courts, and a Frankfurt court went the other way on a medical AI Overview in 2025 ([MLQ](https://mlq.ai/news/aachen-court-extends-german-liability-theory-to-googles-ai-mode/)). Still, the direction is clear. AI is good enough to be trusted and wrong enough to be dangerous, and the law is starting to put that risk on whoever deploys it.

Put the Postgres story beside the Aachen story and the quality problem comes into focus. Machines can now produce the work and even inspect it. What they cannot do is decide what is good and answer for it. That is quality in Pirsig's sense, and it has never lived in the tool; it lives in someone paying attention. The mechanics had the right wrenches. The radio was on.

This is also the part that applies wherever you are in your career, with or without AI. Whatever goes out under your name is your statement. The German courts said it about Google, but clients, employers and judges have always said it about people. The scarce skill is the judgment to know whether something is good before it leaves your hands, and the willingness to stand behind it once it does.

So what do you do with this?

If you are a student or thinking about graduate school, be honest about why you are applying. A degree bought as shelter from a weak market is a bet that the market will want you more in three years, and the evidence is mixed. Entry-level hiring into Am Law 200 firms has been flat for four years, and laterals made up 51% of associate hires ([LawFuel](https://www.lawfuel.com/the-law-graduate-reality-check-shows-record-numbers-fewer-jobs-and-an-ai-shaped-future/)). Whatever you choose, collect proof of judgment. Employers told NACE they want examples of skills used to solve real problems, not lists of skills ([NACE](https://www.naceweb.org/about-us/press/2026/how-new-grad-job-seekers-can-stand-out-with-potential-employers)). Write things yourself, ideally in public, where the quality is visibly yours.

If you are early in your career, become the person who can tell which of the forty findings matter. At Anthropic, the share of employees' AI sessions spent setting up and supervising the AI rose from 10% to 22% in a year ([AI Weekly](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/how-employment-is-changing-at-anthropic)). That supervising work is judgment, and it is where the next generation of seniors will come from. Read every line you forward. Ask why the draft says what it says. A junior who catches the model's error is worth more than one who produced the draft faster.

If you manage people, the training ground is now your problem. If AI writes the first drafts, juniors no longer learn by writing them, so build the reps on purpose: have juniors review, correct and defend AI output, and evaluate the judgment along with the result. In ten years you will need partners and directors who learned what good looks like somewhere. And treat anything you sign off on as your own statement, because courts and clients will.

If you run a business, every answer your AI gives is your company speaking. The German courts drew that line for search, and the reasoning travels to any product that answers questions about real people or companies. Keep a channel for corrections, act on it quickly, and keep a record. In Aachen, a takedown request that sat unanswered became part of the case against the company that ignored it.

A fair reader will push back, and should. Korn Ferry's October survey found that 81% of employers plan to increase early-career hiring over the next two years, and most expect entry-level work to change, not disappear ([Korn Ferry](https://www.stocktitan.net/news/KFY/korn-ferry-report-ai-isn-t-eliminating-the-entry-level-job-it-s-jdo5q4lpw2li.html)). Derek Muller has argued that the market for new lawyers remains robust, with firms still bidding against each other for summer associates ([TaxProf Blog](https://taxprofblog.aals.org/2026/04/05/ai-is-not-yet-disrupting-the-robust-job-market-for-law-school-graduates/)). I read both as consistent with the point. The jobs are being redefined around judgment, which is the skill the old junior work used to teach.

Finally, what to watch. GMAC publishes its 2026 Application Trends Survey in late October, which will show whether business school followed law school's surge ([GMAC](https://www.gmac.com/market-intelligence-and-research/market-research/application-trends-survey)). Big Tech reports earnings on October 28 and 29, and how executives talk about AI efficiency and headcount will say a lot about next year's entry-level classes ([Tech Insider](https://tech-insider.org/ai-hiring-headwinds-goldman-sachs-briggs-10000-jobs-2026/)). PostgreSQL 19 is due around November 19; watch whether other projects start pulling features as AI review raises their bar. And watch whether Google challenges the Aachen injunction, or settles again to keep the question away from a court whose ruling would bind it ([Mad Robot](https://madrobot.blog/2026/10/18/german-courts-google-liable-ai-overviews-ai-mode-aachen-munich-ruling/)).
